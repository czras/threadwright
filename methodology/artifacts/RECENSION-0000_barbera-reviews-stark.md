# David Stark — *The Sense of Dissonance*

## Reflections after Filippo Barbera's review

**David Stark, *The Sense of Dissonance: Accounts of Worth in Economic Life***

These are my reflections after reading Filippo Barbera's review and thinking about its connections to Threadwright. They are not intended as a reconstruction of Stark's argument; they are my own associations triggered by the review.

The review makes me want to read the whole book.

---

## 1. From search to inquiry

There is a fundamental difference between **search** and **inquiry**.

Suppose I have an intent:

> I want to achieve X.

I do not yet know how to realize it. I want to find a realization efficiently, given my values, preferences, constraints and other circumstances.

A conventional search engine helps me search for possible realizations. But there is an important human translation step:

```text
Intent
  ↓
Human formulates search query
  ↓
Search engine retrieves candidates
  ↓
Human interprets results
  ↓
Human constructs a possible realization
```

The human has to translate the intent into a query and then reconstruct something useful from the returned information.

An LLM/agent changes the shape of this interaction.

```text
Intent
  ↓
Human ↔ Agent
  │
  ├── explore possible interpretations
  ├── retrieve information
  ├── reason about possibilities
  ├── expose branches and alternatives
  ├── clarify the intent
  ├── construct candidate realizations
  └── plan possible actions
  ↓
Human judges the candidate space
  ↓
Human decides what to pursue
```

The human does not merely formulate the initial query. The human and agent can **explore the intent itself**.

The agent can help discover that the original intent was underspecified, reveal dimensions of the decision that were initially implicit, and expose multiple branches of possible realization.

The human contributes primarily:

* intent
* values and preferences
* contextual knowledge
* judgment
* decisions
* responsibility for choosing and acting

The agent contributes:

* reasoning
* information retrieval
* memory
* structure
* exploration
* generation of alternatives
* assistance in planning and realization

This looks much more like a **peer-to-peer collaboration** than a traditional information retrieval system.

And this is where Threadwright begins to look like an **inquiry framework in the Deweyan sense**.

---

## 2. The value function is not necessarily numerical

A useful abstraction is the idea of a **value function**:

> Given a set of possible realizations, how do I judge their desirability?

But the value function is really the composition of our values and preferences.

It is not necessarily something that can be reduced to a numerical optimization function.

It may instead consist of:

* preferences
* principles
* constraints
* trade-offs
* contextual considerations
* things we explicitly value
* things we refuse to compromise
* things whose relative importance is itself uncertain

This makes the problem fundamentally different from straightforward optimization.

The interesting question is not simply:

> Which solution maximizes the objective?

but:

> What are the possible realizations, what principles can I use to evaluate them, and what do I learn by keeping several possibilities in play?

---

## 3. Dissonance as productive uncertainty

One of the ideas from the review that immediately connected to Threadwright is Stark's description of entrepreneurship as:

> “the ability to keep multiple principles of evaluation in play and to benefit from that productive friction.”

This strongly resonates with:

> **Generate rich, compress deliberately.**

and more generally:

> **Optimize for flexibility. Keep your options open.**

If uncertainty is high, prematurely collapsing the solution space can be costly.

A single evaluative principle produces a relatively narrow search:

```text
problem
  ↓
criterion
  ↓
optimization
  ↓
solution
```

Keeping multiple principles in play produces something more like:

```text
                    ┌── principle A
                    │
problem ── explore ─┼── principle B
                    │
                    ├── principle C
                    │
                    └── principle D
```

The friction between these perspectives is not necessarily something to eliminate.

It can itself generate new possibilities.

This suggests a connection between Stark's **productive friction** and Threadwright's deliberate preservation of alternatives, uncertainty, competing hypotheses and rejected paths.

The important thing is not to preserve everything forever.

It is to avoid **premature compression** when the uncertainty is still consequential.

---

## 4. Uncertainty is both solved and created

The review describes an interesting contrast with the French conventionalist perspective.

Orders of worth can make action possible by resolving uncertainty.

Stark instead emphasizes how a mixture of evaluative principles can **create uncertainty**, while simultaneously opening opportunities for action.

That produces a particularly interesting formulation:

> **Uncertainty is both solved and created by exploring the unknown.**

Inquiry can reduce one uncertainty while exposing another.

```text
unknown
   ↓
exploration
   ↓
new knowledge
   ↓
previous uncertainty reduced
   ↓
new distinctions become visible
   ↓
new possibilities / contradictions / questions
   ↓
new uncertainty
```

This is very close to how exploratory work actually behaves.

A useful investigation does not necessarily converge monotonically toward certainty.

Sometimes it makes the problem **larger but better understood**.

That is not failure.

It can be progress.

---

## 5. Entrepreneurship as navigating the unknown

This leads to another interpretation of Stark's notion of entrepreneurship.

Entrepreneurship can be understood not merely as starting companies or allocating capital, but as the ability to **navigate an unknown possibility space**.

The skill is partly:

* recognizing possibilities,
* maintaining multiple evaluations,
* exploiting productive disagreement,
* discovering new combinations,
* deciding when to preserve optionality,
* deciding when to commit.

In that interpretation:

> **Entrepreneurship is the act and skill of navigating the unknown.**

That is remarkably close to the behavior I want Threadwright to support.

TW should not tell us what the answer is.

It should help us **explore the unknown while preserving enough of the exploration to understand how we arrived where we are**.

---

## 6. Burt, information and the missing uncertainty

The review's discussion of Burt also triggered a distinction for me.

There is a difference between:

* having more information, and
* encountering something that changes what can be known or done.

Information can fill a gap in an existing conceptual model.

But uncertainty can also arise because the existing model itself is inadequate.

That is where **new knowledge** becomes different from simply acquiring more information.

The missing dimension in a purely information-centric view is therefore:

> **uncertainty about the structure of the possibility space itself.**

This is another reason why an LLM/agent collaboration is interesting.

An agent can generate alternatives that were not present in the human's original representation of the problem.

The interaction can therefore change not only the information available to the human, but the **space of things the human considers possible**.

---

## 7. Heterarchy and distributed intelligence

Another particularly strong connection is:

> **“Heterarchy is the form of distributed intelligence.”**

This immediately suggests a connection to contemporary product organizations.

Stark's observation that innovative firms can benefit from radical decentralization — where virtually every unit becomes engaged in innovation — is very close to Marty Cagan's conception of empowered product teams.

The practical idea is similar:

> Put knowledge, judgment and decision-making close to where the relevant information exists.

Instead of:

```text
central authority
       ↓
instructions
       ↓
execution
```

we get:

```text
distributed knowledge
        ↓
distributed judgment
        ↓
distributed experimentation
        ↓
organizational learning
```

The organization becomes a knowledge-processing system.

The interesting question for HAI is then:

> What happens when the distributed human intelligence of such an organization is coupled with distributed artificial intelligence?

The agent does not replace the human organizational structure.

It potentially becomes another participant in the organization's knowledge-processing and inquiry processes.

---

## 8. The Minotaur case

The Minotaur case in the review caught me for a completely different reason.

Stark discusses Taurus Gumiipari Vállalat.

My father worked at Taurus in Nyíregyháza before moving to TVK, sometime in the 1970s.

So the case suddenly became personally concrete rather than simply sociological.

But the methodological connection is more interesting than the personal one.

The Minotaur case appears as an example of an organization in which different evaluative principles, knowledge and organizational positions interact rather than being cleanly reducible to a single optimization criterion.

That is precisely the sort of environment in which:

> **dissonance can become productive.**

And this reinforces my intuition that TW should not primarily be thought of as a better procedure for executing known processes.

It is more interesting as a way of **exploring the unknown and letting things emerge**.

---

## 9. From procedural algorithms to emergent algorithms

This gives me another possible conceptual progression:

```text
procedural algorithm
        ↓
evolutionary algorithm
        ↓
emergent algorithm
```

A procedural algorithm specifies the path.

An evolutionary algorithm explores a defined space through variation and selection.

An emergent algorithm is different again: the interesting behavior arises from interactions within the system rather than from a fully specified procedure.

I increasingly see Threadwright in this direction.

TW is not intended to prescribe:

> “First do A, then B, then C.”

Instead, it tries to establish a control loop in which the next useful activity emerges from the current state.

```text
desired outcome
       ↓
current state
       ↓
consequential gaps
       ↓
what needs to be learned / decided / changed?
       ↓
useful activity
       ↓
updated state
       ↓
reassessment
       ↺
```

The process is therefore **structured without being fully predetermined**.

That distinction may be important.

---

## 10. Complex performance criteria

The previous sections suggest another connection.

If our evaluation depends on multiple principles simultaneously, then the performance criterion is not a simple scalar objective.

It is a **complex or composite performance criterion**.

This again challenges the idea that inquiry is simply optimization.

The system must navigate a space in which several things may matter simultaneously, sometimes inconsistently.

That makes the preservation of alternatives particularly valuable.

A premature single objective can destroy information about the trade-offs that actually matter.

---

## 11. Stark's three domains

The three studies discussed in the review seem to traverse very different organizational environments:

* production / manufacturing
* IT
* trade / stock markets

What is interesting is their orthogonality.

These are substantially different domains, yet Stark appears to use them to investigate a common underlying organizational phenomenon:

> How can organizations operate productively when multiple evaluative principles, forms of knowledge and sources of uncertainty coexist?

This strengthens the idea that the argument is not fundamentally about one particular industry.

It is about a more general property of organizations as **systems for producing, processing and acting upon knowledge under uncertainty**.

---

## 12. Stark's five directions and Threadwright

The five directions identified at the end of the review provide an especially interesting bridge to Threadwright.

### Search → explore the unknown

Do not treat search as merely retrieving an answer.

Use search and inquiry to expand the space of possible realizations.

This corresponds to the TW principle:

> **Explore the unknown.**

---

### Organizations of diversity → generate rich

If diversity of evaluative principles can create productive friction, then organizations should not optimize too early for consensus.

This corresponds strongly to:

> **Generate rich, compress deliberately.**

Preserve different interpretations and possibilities until there is a reason to collapse them.

---

### Reflexive cognition → TW control loop

The organization must be able to observe its own state and reconsider what it is doing.

This resembles the TW control loop:

```text
state
  ↓
assessment
  ↓
gap
  ↓
activity
  ↓
new state
  ↓
reassessment
```

The system is not merely executing a plan.

It is continuously **reasoning about its own situation**.

---

### Coordination through misunderstanding → reconcile consequential gaps

Misunderstanding is not necessarily noise that should simply be eliminated.

Different actors may have different representations because they occupy different positions and possess different knowledge.

The useful question becomes:

> Which differences matter?

This maps naturally onto the TW concept of **consequential gaps**.

Not every disagreement needs to be resolved.

But consequential disagreement needs to become visible and be dealt with.

---

### Social and economic life → inherently composed

The final direction I take from the review is that social and economic systems are inherently composed of multiple principles, actors, forms of knowledge and evaluative regimes.

Trying to reduce that composition to one universal ordering may destroy exactly the information that makes adaptation and innovation possible.

That is another reason why Threadwright should remain deliberately minimal.

---

## 13. A possible HAI interpretation

Putting the pieces together, I see a broader HAI hypothesis emerging.

Traditional information systems primarily help humans **retrieve and manipulate information**.

LLM/agent systems can potentially participate in **inquiry itself**.

The difference might be expressed as:

```text
Traditional information system

Human intent
    ↓
query
    ↓
information retrieval
    ↓
human interpretation
    ↓
decision
```

versus:

```text
Human–agent inquiry

Human intent
    ↕
agent exploration
    ↕
clarification
    ↕
alternative generation
    ↕
information retrieval
    ↕
reasoning
    ↕
evaluation
    ↓
human judgment
    ↓
decision
```

The human remains the source of intent and judgment.

The agent becomes a participant in the exploration of the possibility space.

This makes the central HAI problem less like:

> **How do humans use AI?**

and more like:

> **How should humans and agents jointly conduct inquiry?**

Threadwright could be viewed as one possible answer to that question.

---

## 14. TW as infrastructure for inquiry

This brings me back to the core idea.

Threadwright may be less interesting as a project-management methodology than as an **infrastructure for inquiry**.

Its persistent artifacts provide memory.

Its relationships provide structure.

Its consequential gaps provide a mechanism for deciding what matters.

Its control loop provides reflexivity.

Its preservation of hypotheses, decisions, rejected paths and uncertainty preserves the history of inquiry.

And its deliberate separation of exploration from premature compression creates room for emergence.

In that sense:

> **TW is a way of making inquiry persistent, inspectable and actionable.**

The agent can participate in the inquiry, but the methodology does not require the agent.

That distinction matters.

TW should remain useful to humans without AI, while also providing a structure in which human–agent collaboration can become much more powerful.

---

## 15. The emerging picture

The strongest connection I currently see is therefore not a one-to-one mapping between Stark and Threadwright.

It is a common picture of action under uncertainty:

```text
                 UNKNOWN
                    │
                    ▼
             EXPLORE / INQUIRE
                    │
          ┌─────────┴─────────┐
          ▼                   ▼
    new information      new possibilities
          │                   │
          └─────────┬─────────┘
                    ▼
             multiple values
             / perspectives
                    │
                    ▼
            productive friction
                    │
                    ▼
          judgment / coordination
                    │
                    ▼
                 action
                    │
                    ▼
              changed state
                    │
                    └──────→ inquire again
```

The goal is not to eliminate uncertainty.

The goal is to **navigate it intelligently**.

And perhaps the deepest connection to Threadwright is this:

> **A good inquiry does not merely find an answer. It changes the space in which the next question can be asked.**

That is exactly the kind of system I want TW to support.

## 16. What I want to investigate next

The review has crossed the threshold from “interesting reference” to:

> **I want to read Stark's whole book.**

In particular, I want to investigate whether the connections above survive contact with Stark's actual argument rather than only with Barbera's review.

Questions to carry into the full reading:

1. How exactly does Stark define and operationalize **dissonance**?
2. What is the relationship between dissonance, uncertainty and innovation?
3. How does **heterarchy** work in practice?
4. How does Stark's account differ from conventional optimization and from conventionalist theories of worth?
5. What exactly does he mean by **reflexive cognition**?
6. How does **coordination through misunderstanding** work?
7. What does Stark's treatment of entrepreneurship imply about exploration versus exploitation?
8. Which parts genuinely resemble Deweyan inquiry?
9. Which connections to Threadwright are substantive, and which are merely analogies?
10. Could Stark provide a sociological/economic theoretical foundation for some of the things TW currently treats as methodological intuitions?

For now, the most useful hypothesis is simple:

> **Threadwright may be an inquiry infrastructure for environments where uncertainty, multiple evaluative principles, distributed knowledge and human–agent collaboration make premature optimization particularly costly.**
